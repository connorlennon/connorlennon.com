Prologue Columbia, SC

A finance kid who liked people more than spreadsheets

I studied finance and international business at the University of South Carolina's Darla Moore School of Business and graduated magna cum laude. I loved the numbers, but I kept noticing that the interesting part was always the people behind them: why they decided what they decided, and what got in their way.

01 2020 · New York, NY

Cold calls in a very strange year

My first job was in sales development at BrightEdge, starting in the spring of 2020. It was a strange time to start anything. My whole job was getting busy strangers to give me a few minutes, and by the end I'd built more than $350K of qualified pipeline.

  • $350K+pipeline built
What it taught me Everyone is busy. Respect their time, and get to the point.
In hindsight My early calls were all pitch. The good ones began once I'd shown I knew their world. Credibility earned me the real question: what matters to you, and can we actually help?

02 2021–2022 · Limerick, PA

Starting something from zero

In 2021 I co-founded Cobblestone Capital Group, a boutique wealth management firm. No clients, no assets, no logo anyone recognized. I earned my Series 7 and 66, did the research, made the calls, and we grew to more than $1.5M under management with 10 new clients, one conversation at a time.

  • $0 → $1.5M+assets under management
  • 10new clients
What it taught me Trust doesn't scale. You earn it one person at a time.
In hindsight Nobody hired us for returns alone. These were busy people who wanted one worry off their plate. Earn the trust, carry the weight, and they get back to what they do best.

03 2023–2025 · Charlotte, NC

Learning where people get stuck

Next came BrightDime, a fintech startup. I started in business development and marketing, doing customer discovery and competitive research that sharpened how we told our story. That brought in $90K+ in new annual recurring revenue, and our content strategy more than doubled website traffic.

Then I moved into customer experience and looked after 50+ business clients. I spent a lot of time in the feedback and usage data, finding exactly where people dropped off during signup and onboarding, then fixing those spots.

  • 98%client retention
  • +10.6%monthly active users
  • +139%website traffic
What it taught me The data tells you where people get stuck. The people tell you why.
In hindsight Nobody stays for a feature list. They stay when the first visit is easy and pays off quickly. Every step we cleared before that first bit of value brought more people back.

04 2025–now · Charlotte, NC

Making a big bank feel a little faster

Today I'm a Senior Consultant (AVP) on Truist's Client Experience and Digital team. Every new digital product idea starts with an intake request, and those requests were waiting more than a month for a decision. So I redesigned the form and the process behind it, added automatic status updates, and built a dashboard so 650+ people could finally see where their requests stood.

I've also helped evaluate around 100 digital product ideas, built dashboards that connect each idea to what actually ships, and piloted a GenAI assistant that reads new requests, spots what's missing and drafts the follow-up questions, with a human always making the call.

What it's teaching me The slow part is rarely where the problem starts.
In hindsight The slow decisions were a symptom. The cause sat upstream, in what we asked for on day one. Fix the first step and everything after it runs smoother.

Notes on stuck spots

Four short ones, on things I keep noticing.

Credibility comes before curiosity1 min

Ask a busy person what matters most to them and you'll usually get the polite answer. The real one comes later, and only once they've decided you're worth telling.

What changes their mind is rarely a claim about yourself. It's a detail. You mention something specific about their business, their week, the thing their competitor just did, and you can watch them recalibrate. You've done the reading. Now your questions sound like interest and not a script.

The same thing happens outside sales. A user interview goes deeper once the person can tell you've used the product. A stakeholder opens up once you can describe their problem in their own words. Credibility isn't the point of the conversation. It's the price of admission to the useful part, where you find out what they need and whether you can honestly help with it.

Take something off their plate1 min

The people most worth working with tend to have the least spare attention. They're good at one thing, and everything else in their life competes with it.

So the offer that lands is seldom "here is more." More information, more options, more meetings are all things they now have to manage. The offer that lands is "here is one thing you no longer have to think about." A decision already narrowed to two choices. A summary they can trust without checking. A problem that gets handled and reported, not escalated.

Trust builds the same way, in small deposits. You say what you'll do, you do it, and you make it easy to see that it's done. After enough of those, people stop checking, and that's the real gift: they get their attention back for the work only they can do.

The first few minutes decide the rest1 min

Watch someone use a product for the first time and you can usually tell whether they'll be back. It has little to do with how much the product can do. It comes down to how long it takes before it does something for them.

Every step in front of that moment is a toll: the account to create, the form to finish, the tour to click through, the setting nobody explained. Each one is reasonable on its own. Together they add up to a person doing work on faith, and faith runs out fast.

The experiences people return to tend to flip the order. They give you something first, then ask for what they need. Easier is part of it, but it isn't the whole of it. The first visit should leave someone a little better off than they arrived, quickly enough that they notice. That's what brings them back.

Fix it upstream1 min

Most recurring problems are downstream of something. The late decision started as an unclear request. The support ticket started as a confusing screen. The meeting that keeps getting scheduled started as a question nobody wrote down the answer to.

It's tempting to get very good at handling the symptom. Faster responses, better templates, a dashboard of everything that's overdue. All of that is real work and it does help, but the queue keeps refilling because nothing has changed where the work comes from.

The better question is what had to be true for this to happen at all, asked again at each step until you reach a spot where one small change removes the whole category. That fix is usually modest and unglamorous: a clearer question on a form, a default that's right most of the time. Change it once and everything after it gets easier.
